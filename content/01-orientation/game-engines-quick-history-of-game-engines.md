# Game Engines: Quick history of game engines

The importance of video game home consoles in entertainment across the 1970s, and into the 1980s, introduced a need for a "framework" or collection of "software libraries" to solve common problems for developers and help standardize operations. From into the late 1980s and early 1990s, larger companies began to standardize around certain approaches. One of these, id Software, created a collection of software they named id Tech Engine as part of their work on one of the most popular first-per-shooter *Doom* (1993).

Important for the history of game engines is the rise of creative differences between the original group that created *Doom* (1993) during their development of what would become *Quake* *(1996)*. While id Tech became popular (and gave rise to modifications, "mods", popularizing many forms of multiplayer FPS play), it also struggled to market its technology. While it was used for later popular series like *Call of Duty* (2003) and its sequels, two other companies began to fight in the same space.

### Rise of Epic and Unreal Engine

Epic Games, originally Epic MegaGames, created the first version of its own game engine, Unreal, for a game they were developing named after the tool itself, *Unreal* (1998). After the success of the game, they began to license out the engine for other games and improving it to Unreal Engine 2. This used for popular games like *Tom Clancy's Splinter Cell* (2002), *Deus Ex: Invisible War* (2003, and later *BioShock* (2007).

Following profit sharing success (where Epic made money from the licensing of the engine), they invested in improving it, introducing the visual scripting programming language Blueprints in 2014 alongside Unreal Engine 4. Epic also introduced a new licensing deal for developers, opening its usage to more people.

While Unreal Engine 3 (2006) was popular, Unreal Engine 4 (2014), based on the success of Blueprints and new licensing, opened the door to major usage across entertainment including for popular video games, virtual production, and usage in TV and film.

### Rise of Unity

At roughly the same time as the introduction of Unreal Engine 2 (circa 2002), a group of friends worked on a game engine eventually named *Unity* in June 2005 to run on macOS systems. Its programming interface was initially designed around a version of JavaScript.

While useful, the JavaScript version of its programming interface limited some possible games and also introduced problems, leading to the investigation of other possible languages to use.

From roughly 2000 through 2011, an open source group worked to implement Microsoft's programming language C# for more open operating systems like Linux. By 2016, because it had grown in popularity, Microsoft bought the parent company funding the project.

For Unity, the popularity of Mono created an opportunity to use a well-known programming language, C#, without licensing it from Microsoft. It was first added as an additional language available alongside JavaScript (later known as UnityScript for Unity) and became official in 2017.

### *Where are we now?*

Unity is very popular for independent projects and uses C#. However, in 2023, Unity tried to adopt a new licensing deal that upset many within its community.

Unreal has risen in popularity because of changes in licensing starting in 2014 and the inclusion of Blueprints. It has been and continues to be used to create AAA video game projects.

While Unity and Unreal are major players, they are not the only ones. Godot, another game engine, became more available in 2014. Many companies also use their own game engines like EA's Frostbite engine and Sony's PhyreEngine, which was used from 2007 to 2020 for PlayStation 3, PlayStation 4, and PSP systems.
